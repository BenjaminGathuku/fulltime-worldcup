The critics said 48 teams would dilute it. They said the round of 32 was padding. They said a tournament spread across three nations and sixteen cities would feel fragmented, rootless, too big for anyone to hold in their arms.

The 2026 World Cup ran 39 days, produced 104 matches, and ended with what many are already calling one of the finest Spain sides ever assembled lifting a trophy they never looked like losing. The critics were wrong about all of it.

Here is the tournament in full -- what it was, what it delivered, and what it leaves behind.

## The new format, vindicated

The expanded format was supposed to benefit the big nations: more games, more safe passage, more cushion before the knockouts bit. Instead it produced the round's defining moments from the other direction. Germany, four-time champions, went out to Paraguay on penalties in the round of 32 in Foxborough. The Netherlands, ranked fourth in the world, fell to Morocco on penalties in Monterrey the following day. Two European heavyweights, eliminated in a single weekend, in a round that hadn't existed four weeks earlier.

The format's critics had said the early rounds would be hollow. The format's greatest achievement was making them explosive.

## The tournament's soul: Cape Verde

If you need one image to carry the spirit of 2026, let it be this: a goalkeeper named Vozinha, 40 years old, playing club football in the Portuguese second division, standing between Lamine Yamal and a goal at a FIFA World Cup. The Blue Sharks of Cape Verde -- half a million people, their first tournament -- drew Spain, drew Uruguay, drew Saudi Arabia, and then held Argentina to extra time in the round of 32 before Lisandro Martínez finally broke them in the 112th minute.

They were the smallest nation ever to reach the World Cup knockout stage. They left unbeaten in normal time across four matches. No other story from 39 days of football quite captures what the game does when it is at its generous best.

## The controversy that wouldn't close

Argentina's path to the final was the tournament's great debate. Cape Verde in extra time. Egypt leading 2–0 with 11 minutes left before Romero, Messi and Fernández completed a resurrection -- after VAR had, minutes earlier, disallowed an Egyptian goal for contact that occurred on the opposite side of the pitch. Switzerland dragging them to the 112th minute of a quarter-final. England leading them in the semi-final with five minutes left before two goals in seven minutes settled it.

The Egyptian FA issued a formal statement. Argentina fans called it destiny. Neutrals called it both, and neither, and argued. The champions were fortunate, unquestionably. They were also, equally unquestionably, never beaten when it mattered. Champions find ways. Scaloni's Argentina found four of them in succession.

## The records that stood at full time

Kylian Mbappé: 22 World Cup career goals, the all-time men's record, broken in the third-place match in Miami at age 27. Lionel Messi: 20 World Cup goals across 30 appearances at six tournaments, now second on the all-time list. Unai Simón: 650 consecutive World Cup minutes without conceding, a new record. Jude Bellingham: seven World Cup goals, the highest ever by an England player. Lamine Yamal: a World Cup winner's medal at 19, less than 24 hours after his birthday.

The first 48-team World Cup produced more records than any since 1970.

## Spain: the answer to the question about greatness

Eight matches. Eight wins. One goal conceded. They beat their group opponents comfortably, suffocated France without breaking a sweat, defeated Belgium in the cruelest fashion through a substitute goalkeeper's error and a substitute's rebound, and then won a final against the world champions in extra time with ten men against them.

The debate that runs into 2030 is whether this Spain -- organised around Rodri's governance of midfield, Yamal's fearlessness on the right, Williams' directness on the left, and a defensive structure that turned Simón's job into administration -- belongs alongside the 2008-2012 generation that won two Euros and a World Cup, or stands apart from it. The case for apart: this squad did what that generation never managed, winning a World Cup in the Americas, at a 48-team expanded edition, against the most decorated squad in the tournament's history. The case against: 2008-2012 changed football. The jury remains out on whether 2026 does the same.

What is not in dispute: they were the best team, they deserved the trophy, and they never once looked as though someone else might take it.

## Messi's last page

He scored eight goals. He made assists that left defenders standing. He rescued Argentina from the dead in Atlanta with an assist so precise it was almost insulting. He stood in the MetLife rain afterwards, silver medal around his neck, and conducted himself with the grace that has followed him through everything football put his way.

The World Cup is over for Lionel Messi. He leaves as the sport's second-highest scorer in tournament history, the record-holder for appearances, the man who brought Argentina their first title in 36 years in 2022 and came within a single goal of matching Brazil's 1958-1962 back-to-back achievement in 2026. He never won a World Cup final without needing extra time. He never played one that wasn't genuinely important. He never wasted a summer.

The question of whether he was the greatest player who ever lived is, at this point, the most boring question in football. The answer is yes, and has been for a decade. Messi's World Cup career asks a different and more interesting question: how do you measure a man who achieved the impossible once, then nearly did it again, at 39?

## The legacy of 2026

The tournament will be remembered for its scale -- 16 cities across three nations, from Vancouver to Miami, from Mexico City to Boston -- and for the proof that scale need not mean shapelessness. The Azteca staged its third World Cup. MetLife hosted the final with the capacity and noise of a stadium that felt built for exactly this purpose. The logistics that pundits feared would fracture the tournament instead gave it a continental grandeur that may define how future editions are conceived.

It will be remembered for its football: ten goals between England and France in a third-place match; Argentina's four escapes; Spain's serene eight-match procession; Mbappé scoring twenty-two times before his twenty-eighth birthday; Saka converting a penalty five years after missing one became the defining image of English football.

And it will be remembered as the last act of one era and the confident opening of another. Messi's World Cup career is finished. Ronaldo's finished three weeks before the final. The age of the two men who dominated football's last decade is over, and the stage now belongs to Mbappé, Bellingham, Yamal and those just behind them.

Four years to 2030. Another 48 teams. The conversation starts again tomorrow.
