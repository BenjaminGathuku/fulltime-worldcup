The last time a player this young changed a World Cup final, people spent decades talking about it. Lamine Yamal is not Pele. He is not trying to be. He is something different, something specific to this moment in football, a player who arrived so early and with such complete authority over his position that the sport has had to recalibrate its understanding of what a teenage footballer can do.

He is 19 years old. He just won the World Cup. He plays for the best team in La Liga. He finished second in last year's Ballon d'Or vote. The case for him is not about what he might become. It is about what he has already done.

## The World Cup: the argument that settles debates

The Ballon d'Or has, since its expansion to cover international football as part of the evaluation criteria, consistently rewarded players whose club excellence was validated by success on the biggest international stage. The World Cup is that stage. There is no equivalent in football, no competition that carries the same weight of history, expectation and scrutiny.

Yamal played the full 120 minutes of the World Cup final as Spain beat Argentina. He was not a peripheral figure managed carefully through the knockout rounds, appearing for cameos and conserved for crucial moments. He was central, active, and impactful throughout Spain's run. His presence in that final, his ability to perform at 19 under the specific pressure of a World Cup decider, is not something that statistical comparison can fully capture.

The voters have the final in their minds. They watched it. They watched a teenager from Barcelona play the full match at the highest level the sport offers, and come out on the winning side. That image, that fact, carries weight in a ballot that is as much about impression as measurement.

## The club campaign: a La Liga season for the ages

Before the World Cup, Yamal had already made his case for the award at club level. Barcelona's La Liga title under Hansi Flick was built on a collective system, but Yamal's contribution to that system was irreplaceable. Twenty-four goals and seventeen assists in forty-five appearances across the campaign produced a Player of the Season award that the vote reflected unanimously.

Those numbers require some unpacking. Seventeen assists in a single season is an extraordinary creative return. Yamal is not just scoring; he is making the players around him better, finding passes in spaces that most wingers do not see, and doing so with a consistency that belies his age. The assist total, in particular, speaks to a player whose game is about more than individual brilliance. He elevates those around him, which is precisely what the Ballon d'Or, at its best, tries to reward.

Playing the full 120 minutes of a World Cup final while also producing that kind of club season requires a physical and mental resilience that is genuinely unusual at any age. At 19, it is almost without precedent.

## The historical weight: the youngest winner ever

If Yamal wins on 26 October, he will become the youngest Ballon d'Or winner in the award's history. That is a record that will stand for a very long time. The youngest previous winners were players who had already spent years at the highest level before the award recognised them. Yamal arrived at that recognition faster than anyone before him.

There is a legitimate concern that youth and potential should not override the specific achievements of any given reference period, and it is a concern worth taking seriously. The Ballon d'Or is not a lifetime achievement award or a prize for the most exciting player to watch. It is supposed to reflect the best player over a specific timeframe.

But Yamal's case is not built on potential. He was, by the standard of the votes already cast for him (runner-up last year), already considered one of the two best players in the world. This year he won what last year's winner Dembele did not have: a World Cup medal. He added the one piece of evidence that separated him from the top spot in 2025.

## The comparison with Kane: what the voters are really deciding

Harry Kane scored more goals. That is the central fact of the Kane argument and it should not be dismissed. Thirty-six Bundesliga goals and six World Cup goals is a return that defines elite striking. But goals and the Ballon d'Or have a complicated relationship.

The award has consistently, over its history, weighted a certain kind of influence above raw numbers. Messi won eight of them, and the years he won were not always the years he scored the most. The voters tend to favour players whose impact feels defining, whose presence shaped the outcomes that mattered most.

Yamal shaped Spain's World Cup. He was present, central, and effective across a tournament that Spain won. Kane scored more goals in a tournament that England lost. Both facts are true. The question is which fact is more decisive, and it is a question that the history of the award suggests will resolve in favour of the player on the winning side.

## The honest case: it is probably his award

The betting markets have moved toward Yamal at 10/11. That movement reflects something real. The journalists who vote on this award have watched both players over the reference period, have cast their votes, and the market's assessment of how that vote will resolve leans toward the teenager from Barcelona.

If he wins, it will not be an upset. It will be the recognition of a player who won La Liga, won the World Cup, and produced twenty-four goals and seventeen assists across a season that established him, finally and definitively, as the best player in the world.

At 19. The youngest ever. A World Cup winner. A La Liga champion. The Ballon d'Or, if it goes to Lamine Yamal on 26 October at the London Palladium, will have gone to exactly the right player.
