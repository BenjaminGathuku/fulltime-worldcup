Nobody wanted to be there. Both coaches admitted it. Half the starters were rested. Didier Deschamps had already announced he was leaving after 14 years. The match-day energy at Hard Rock Stadium had the unmistakable tinge of a party nobody booked but everyone ended up at.

By the 18th minute, England led 2–0. By half-time, it was 4–0. By the hour mark, France had made it 4–3 and the match suddenly had a pulse. By the 90th minute, it had ten goals, a hat-trick, a record, a farewell, and a Bellingham stoppage-time seal that briefly made you forget both teams were supposed to be in tomorrow's final instead.

England 6, France 4. The third-place match this World Cup did not deserve was the one it absolutely needed.

## The first half nobody predicted

Tuchel rested Kane, Bellingham and Pickford. Deschamps sat Barcola and Dembélé, leaving Mbappé to carry France's evening alongside Olise. In theory it was a match without its biggest names. In practice, England opened as if they had points to prove against the team Spain had humiliated 2–0 in the semi-final.

Declan Rice set the tone in the third minute, picking up the ball thirty yards out, noticing Maignan slightly off his line, and launching a strike into the top corner. The finish had conviction. So did Ezri Konsa's 18th-minute header from Rice's own corner, the Aston Villa defender rising unmarked at the far post. At 2–0, France tried to settle.

Bukayo Saka did not let them. His 36th-minute goal came from a sharp break, finishing low past Maignan from the edge of the box. Then, in first-half stoppage time, after Marcus Rashford's shot was beaten away, Saka was first to the rebound. 4–0. Deschamps' half-time team talk, by his own description, was unprintable.

## Mbappé and the comeback

Four second-half substitutions and a different France walked out for the restart. Mbappé, held quiet through forty-five minutes, finally caught fire with two goals in twenty minutes that remind you, on any given occasion, that he is beyond argument the most dangerous forward alive.

His 46th-minute finish was composed; his 66th was clinical, a sharp turn and low drive that made it 4–3 and sent the French sections of the stadium briefly berserk. Bradley Barcola, on as a substitute, added a fourth from the tightest angle in the 54th minute. England's two-goal lead suddenly felt like a thread.

But Saka completed his hat-trick with an 84th-minute penalty, calm amid the noise, and Jude Bellingham arrived off the bench to slide home a sixth in the 90th minute for England, his seventh goal of the tournament. Dembélé pulled one back for France in the seventh minute of stoppage time as a final act of stubbornness. The whistle blew on 6–4.

## The record and the farewell

Buried inside those forty-five second-half minutes, history was made quietly. Mbappé's two goals carried him to 22 World Cup career goals, passing Lionel Messi's mark of 20 to become the men's all-time record holder. He finished as Golden Boot winner with 10 tournament goals, the first man ever to win that award at two consecutive World Cups, and he did it in a match France technically lost.

For Deschamps, the result was painful but the farewell was fitting: one last comeback attempt, one last Mbappé brace, one last defiant French second half in a tournament of defiant French second halves. He led France to the 2018 title, two finals and now a third-place bronze, across fourteen years. As exits go, 6–4 in Miami is at least not boring.

For England, third place was their best World Cup finish since 1966. The bronze medal sits alongside the European Championship bronze from 2024, and the question it leaves behind is whether a generation that went semi-final, final, semi-final, semi-final across four major tournaments under three managers will ever hold the last trophy. It has come close enough that the answer can no longer be assumed.

The real answer will wait until 2030. For now: ten goals in Miami, a record broken, a legend departed, and a crowd that arrived barely interested and left talking about everything they'd seen.
